Privacy policy

Effective 30 October 2026. %BRAND% is a free, non-commercial card game at %DOMAIN%, run by NeuEra Apps LLC ("we"). You play without an account, a name or an email address. This page lists everything the game keeps about you, why, and for how long, and it describes the game as it runs today, not as it might one day.

What the game keeps

  • A player and its handle. The first time a device opens the game it becomes a player: an id and a handle the game makes up (like quiet-otter-42), with the time it was made. The handle is what the other players at your table see. There is no name, email or password.
  • A session. Your browser holds one cookie, which keeps the device signed in as its player. The server keeps a hash of it, never the cookie itself, and when it expires: a year after you last played. Signing out ends it. The cookie is the only one the game sets, and it is needed for the game to work.
  • Your games. Each game is a record of everything played in it: the seats (handles, and any names you typed for people playing at your device), which player holds which seat, every pack and op, the commit messages you wrote, and when each happened. Rooms keep their invite code, their host and who joined, and when. This record is the game: it is how a game is shown, resumed a week later and replayed.
  • Reminders, if you ask for them. If you tap "remind me" and your browser allows notifications, the game keeps the address your browser's push service gave it (an endpoint at Google, Mozilla or Apple, depending on the browser) and the two keys that go with it. A reminder carries no message: it only wakes the game. The game also records each reminder it sent: to which player, about which game and day, and when, so it never sends the same one twice.
  • Feedback you write. When a game ends, the scoreboard has a box for telling us how it went. What you write is kept with your player and the game, and read by the maintainer.
  • Visits. The game notes, once a day, that a player opened it, and whether they came from a reminder; and that a replay was opened. These are how the beta tells whether people come back (ADR-0002), counted by the maintainer, never shown to anyone else.
  • Your IP address, for an hour, in memory. To stop a script making a million players, the server counts how many players each IP address makes in an hour. The count lives in the server's memory, is never written to the database or the logs, and is gone within the hour, or sooner if the server restarts.
  • On your device. The game remembers, in your browser's local storage, which days of a game you have already watched and whether your first game is guided. That never leaves the device.

What it doesn't

No analytics, no advertising, no tracking pixels, and no scripts from anyone else: the pages load only from %DOMAIN%. No name, email address, age, location, contacts or payment details. There is nothing to buy, so there is no billing record. We don't sell or rent anything about you, and we don't share it with anyone for their own use.

Two features are built but switched off on %DOMAIN%, so the game holds nothing for them: linking a GitHub account (which would keep your GitHub id, login and avatar address, never your email or a token) and reminders by email (which would keep the address you give). If either is switched on, this page will say so, and what it keeps, before it is.

Who can see what

The players at your table see your handle and everything you play, including your commit messages. Anyone with a game's link can watch it and replay it as the table saw it, and anyone with a room's link sees who has joined it. A hidden card stays hidden from everyone but you. Nobody but the maintainer sees feedback, visits or reminders.

Who else is involved

  • Fly.io hosts the game and its database, in the United States. As with any host, their network sees each request's IP address, and their logs follow their own policy. The game's own logs record what was asked for (like GET /api/games/…), not who asked.
  • Your browser's push service (Google, Mozilla or Apple), only if you turned on reminders. It delivers an empty push to your browser and learns nothing from us beyond that one was sent.

How long it is kept

A session expires a year after you last played, and can no longer sign anyone in. A reminder subscription goes when your browser's push service says it is no longer there: block notifications for %DOMAIN% and the next reminder is refused, and the subscription deleted. The rate-limit count goes within the hour. Everything else (players, games, rooms, feedback, visits and the record of reminders sent) is kept for as long as the game runs, because a game is a record that the other players at the table share and can replay, or until you ask us to delete it.

Asking for it, or deleting it

Write to hello@neuera.app with your handle, which the game's start screen shows. We will send you what the game holds about your player, or delete it: the player, its sessions, reminders, feedback and visits. In games other people played with you, your seat stays so their game still adds up, under a handle that no longer leads to anything; we will remove a commit message you ask about. We may ask you to confirm from the device you play on that the handle is yours, and we answer within 30 days.

Wherever you live, you can ask for this, and you can complain to your data protection authority if we don't answer. Where the law asks for a basis: we keep what the game needs to run and to stay safe from abuse because a game can't be played without it (our legitimate interest, and yours in playing), and reminders because you asked for them (your consent, which blocking notifications withdraws).

Children

%BRAND% is meant for people aged 13 and over, and asks nobody's age, because it collects nothing that says who you are. If you think a child wrote something personal in a commit message or in feedback, write to us and we will delete it.

Changes

When what the game keeps changes, this page changes first, with a new date at the top. Every earlier version is in the history of this page, beside the code it describes, which is open source. The terms say what you and we agree to.
